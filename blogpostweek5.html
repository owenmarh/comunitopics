Constituents of a theory of the media by Hans Magnus Enzensberger

In Hans Magnus Enzensberger's conception of a traditional media, there is typically a producer and a viewer. The producer communicates an idea and the viewer receives it. Enzensberger's Constituents of a theory of the media complicates this relationship, positing a theory of the media that lays out an 'emancipatory use of media' in contrast to its traditional use -- a use Enzensberger calls 'repressive.' The key elements of this emancipatory use include the following: a decentralized program; each receiver (viewer) also being a potential transmitter; mobilization of the masses; feedback/interaction of those involved; a political learning process; collective production; and social control by self-organization (Enzensberger 26).

Another key point of Enzensberger's essay is that the Marxist Left is lagging behind in their theory of the media and that, as a political necessity, they should argue and act from "the standpoint of the most advanced productive forces in their society...[developing] in depth all the liberating factors immanent in these forces" (Enzensberger 27). From the point at which Enzensberger writes, the 'most advanced productive forces' included television, news satellites, cassettes, videotape, printing, computers...you get the idea. Today, of course, AI as a form of media expands this list. Yet using the 'most advanced productive forces' in society today certainly should not include that which surveils and pacifies the user. Despite the conditions which dictate its use, AI may, however, have immense potential for democracy -- as a tool for connection, participation, and education -- rather than for the pacification of the masses and the exploitation of the earth. The problem is that such emancipatory conditions do not currently exist. Which makes me wonder: could people, together, create an ecosystem allowing for connection, participation, education, and mobilization -- completely outside of the monopoly conditions of AI, and media as a whole?

Initiatives like NYC Mesh suggest that it is already being done. Community media centers too. Public access television allows for the conditions of such freedom -- though these conditions are being eroded. The question undergirding Enzensberger's piece in the modern day, then, is: how do we communicate freely?

As of now, we still have a ways to go until a global network of, for instance, democratic media ecosystems like NYC Mesh grow strong enough to replace the monopolies Enzensberger speaks of which have grown exponentially since his essay was published in 1970. Yet we see break-throughs. The 'most advanced productive forces,' in today's society, then, may not be found in the conventional technological advancements of AI at all, but rather the democratic and anti-hierarchical tools for multi-directional communication that emerge from collective social organization.
