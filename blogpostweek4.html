Claire Bishop's account of the historic avant-garde in her book Artificial Hells, named after Andre Breton's Artificial Hells. Inauguration of the 1921 Dada Season, may share the same genealogy as its precursor. In Breton's Artificial Hells, he describes the events of Dada Season as a "'discussion on moral grounds'...and hints that...it would not be surprising to find the group 'in art, philosophy, or politics'" (p. 73). Bishop, drawing from another person's account of Dada's evolution, conveys that "Dada now presumed to judge rather than simply to negate; in other words, it attempted to find a position rather than offering an a priori rejection of all positions" (73). Bishop's account of the historic avant-garde seems to hint at a similar trajectory - from criticism to construction.

"In short, morality was making inroads," Bishop continues.

In the reading, Bishop traces the evolution of Paris Dada from absurdist and united against thing(s) rather than for. Paris Dada is at first presented as akin to Futurism in its press strategy -- whose history is much darker (read: fascism) than that of Dada. Then, as evidenced by Breton's Barres Trial, which staged a public hearing that called into question Maurice Barres's movement away from anarchist ideology (that had a great deal of influence on Breton and his colleagues) in favor of conservatism, Paris Dada took on a more activist stature with specific goal(s) in mind, in contrast to its initial formation as a movement against nationalism, the state, etc.

Bishop's essay traces the genealogy, in chronological order, of Italian futurist theatre, Russian Proletkult, and Dada participatory art. In this order, we see movement from performance that incites complete chaos with no meaning beyond glorifying violence - and thus, no moral posture - to an overtly moral theater in Russian Proletkult, whose execution, coming from the state, unintentionally exposed a gap between the myth and glorification of Russian communism versus its much gloomier, actual lived reality. Finally, in Paris Dada, things begin to stabilize, with Breton's movement toward a constructive morality that is not state-led nor corrupt (at least from his perspective). From negation to construction.

Admittedly I'm not versed in modern-day participatory art. But I do wonder if there are certain best practices today that combine the principle of being united against something with being united for something else. The fact of the matter is that, as participatory artists, we should not have to choose. For instance, I wonder how the form of a work might be enough to take a position of negation against oppression, while the content of it produces something generative and tangible.

Inspired by Dada were the Situationists, whose position may be closer to what I suggest above. But unfortunately their movement was rife with elitist misogyny, the shadow of which still hangs over much art today. Excited to learn more about different approaches.
