Party Artist: Emory Douglas and the Black Panther Party by Nicolas Lampert (2015)

In this week's reading, I was fascinated by the Black Panther Party's organizational and aesthetic evolution from a group foregrounding armed self-defense to one foregrounding community survival programs. Emory Douglas's images gave this evolution an aesthetic dimension. Beginning with drawings of pigs being gunned down by members of the BPP to drawings of participants holding groceries and clothing from the BPP's community survival programs, advocating for electoral change ("VOTE FOR SURVIVAL"), the change in BPP priorities was reflected directly in its visual culture (Lampert 209). As BPP policy shifted from armed self-defense to community survival, by the spring of 1971, "guns were close to absent in [Douglas's] images" (Lampert 208).

In 1969, the FBI sent a memo to a number of their field offices that described the BPP's free breakfast programs as the "'best and most influential activity going for the BPP and as such...potentially the greatest threat to efforts by authorities...to neutralize the BPP and destroy what it stands for'" (Lampert 209).

I am particularly fascinated by this quote because of its implications for movements today. Through their community survival programs, the BPP provided public welfare when the government did not. The free breakfast programs must have met a particularly acute need given the prevalence of food insecurity that persists today.

To 'neutralize' the threat, then, might the government have tried to coopt such programs through developing their own? If such a program might have been institutionalized, would it have been a net good, and consistent with the BPP's goals? Or solely a cooptation, whose nature would reorganize the BPP's program from one of community organization to one of dependency on the state?

Though cooptation was always a risk, the culture of images that the BPP and Emory Douglas created could not be destroyed. As the BPP was weakened by a variety of FBI tactics, "the culture of the BPP could not be as easily destroyed" (Lampert 210). Emory Douglas's images, then, live on as instructive of a people's ability to defend and take care of themselves when the state does not. In present-day, these images provide reminders of what could be different, and open up possibilities as to how.
